### Testing Ground for Messaging and Products

One of the most valuable (and often overlooked) benefits of event participation is the **real-time market research** you gain.

When you're running Facebook ads, you see metrics: click-through rates, cost per click, conversion rates. Those numbers tell you *what* is happening, but not *why*. You don't know why someone didn't click. You don't know what objection prevented them from purchasing. You don't know what question they had that your website didn't answer.

At events, you get the "why."

**Messaging Testing**:
- When you explain "31:33 refers to Jeremiah 31:33," do people immediately recognize the verse, or do you need to explain further?
- Does the word "belonging" resonate emotionally, or does it fall flat?
- When you say "conversation starter about faith," do women's faces light up, or do they look confused?
- What questions do people ask most frequently? (Those questions reveal gaps in your marketing materials.)

**Product Testing**:
- Which pieces do people gravitate toward first?
- Do they prefer the minimalist "31:33" numbering, or do they want more explicit Christian symbols?
- Are they drawn to sterling silver or gold? Diamonds or plain metal?
- Do they want longer or shorter chain lengths?
- Are there products they wish you offered but don't?

**Pricing Testing**:
- At what price point do you see hesitation in their eyes?
- Do they perceive the jewelry as "expensive" or "investment-quality"?
- Are they comparing your prices to costume jewelry or fine jewelry?
- Would payment plans help with higher-ticket items?

**Packaging and Presentation Testing**:
- Does your booth display attract attention, or do people walk past?
- Do they pick up pieces to examine them, or do they just look from a distance?
- What signage or messaging makes them stop and engage?
- Does your packaging feel premium enough for the price point?

Every conversation at your booth is a mini focus group. By the end of a weekend conference, you'll have talked to 50-200 potential customers and gathered insights that would cost tens of thousands of dollars to obtain through formal market research.

### How This Differs from Other Marketing Channels

It's important to understand where conferences and events fit in your overall marketing strategy and how they differ from the other channels outlined in this Marketing Action Plan.

**vs. Affiliate & Referral Partnerships**:
- **Affiliate programs** are passive and scalable—they run in the background with minimal ongoing effort once set up.
- **Events** are active and concentrated—they require significant preparation, attendance, and follow-up for specific dates.
- **Affiliate programs** prioritize breadth (many partners promoting to many audiences).
- **Events** prioritize depth (intensive engagement with concentrated audience).
- **Use both**: Events can help you recruit affiliates (meet bloggers/influencers at conferences), and affiliates can drive traffic to your event booth (they announce you'll be there).

**vs. Micro-Influencer Partnerships**:
- **Micro-influencer partnerships** leverage others' audiences and credibility.
- **Events** leverage your own presence and Dana's personal testimony.
- **Micro-influencer content** reaches people where they already are (scrolling social media).
- **Events** require people to come to you (but they're already gathered and receptive).
- **Use both**: Events are excellent places to meet and recruit micro-influencers in person.

**vs. Podcast Appearances**:
- **Podcasts** offer extended storytelling time (30-60 minutes) to share Dana's testimony and brand story.
- **Events** offer brief interactions (2-5 minutes per person) but with hundreds of people.
- **Podcasts** build awareness and interest that drives people to your website.
- **Events** convert interest into immediate sales.
- **Use both**: Mention upcoming events during podcast appearances to drive booth traffic.

**vs. Organic Social Media**:
- **Social media** is ongoing, daily presence-building.
- **Events** are periodic, intensive sales opportunities.
- **Social media** requires consistent content creation and community management.
- **Events** generate content that fuels your social media for weeks.
- **Use both**: Use social media to promote event attendance, then share event content on social media afterward.

**vs. Email Marketing**:
- **Email** nurtures relationships over time with people who've already expressed interest.
- **Events** create those initial relationships and capture email addresses.
- **Email** is one-to-many communication.
- **Events** are one-to-one conversations that build deeper connection.
- **Use both**: Collect emails at events, then nurture those contacts via email campaigns.

The key insight: **Events aren't a replacement for other channels—they're a catalyst**. Events generate the customers, content, testimonials, relationships, and market intelligence that make all your other marketing channels more effective.

If you spend $5,000 on Facebook ads, you might generate 15-20 sales (if you're lucky and your targeting is perfect). You'll get some data on click-through rates and conversion rates, but you won't know *why* people didn't convert. You won't have customer testimonials. You won't have photos of real women wearing your jewelry. You won't have made any personal connections. And if the ads don't work, that $5,000 is gone with little to show for it.

If you spend $5,000 to attend a regional Christian women's conference (booth fee, travel, lodging, marketing materials), you'll likely:

- Collect 100+ email addresses for future marketing
- Capture dozens of photos and testimonials
- Make 5-10 valuable business connections
- Gain deep insights into your target customer
- Build confidence in your product-market fit

Even if the event itself only breaks even financially, the **non-financial ROI** (relationships, content, market research, confidence) makes it worthwhile.

That said, you don't need to start with $5,000 events. The beauty of the conference and events channel is that it's **infinitely scalable** based on your budget and risk tolerance.

**Starting Small (Under $500 total investment)**:

- Small regional women's retreats ($200-400 booth fees)

At this level, you're testing the concept with minimal financial risk. Even if you only sell 10-15 pieces, you've validated that in-person sales work and gathered valuable feedback.

**Scaling Regionally ($1,000-3,000 per event)**:

**Going National ($5,000-15,000 per event)**:

At this level, you're making a serious investment, but you're also accessing thousands of potential customers and establishing 31:33 as a recognized brand in the Christian women's space. This is where you go once you've proven the model at smaller events and have the inventory and systems to handle high-volume sales.

**The Strategic Approach**: Start small, prove the concept, scale based on results. Don't jump straight to a $10,000 national conference booth if you've never sold jewelry at an event before. Test at 3-5 local/regional events first. Learn what works. Refine your approach. Build confidence. *Then* make the bigger investments.

### Inventory Allocation Strategy

One concern you raised in the strategy session was inventory management: "Does it make sense for me to do a three-month marketing campaign with only 45 items in stock?"

Events actually solve this problem rather than exacerbate it.

Here's why:

**Concentrated Sales Opportunity**: Instead of hoping to sell 45 pieces over three months of online marketing, you could potentially sell 30-40 of them in a single weekend at a well-attended conference. That's faster inventory turnover, which means faster cash flow to reinvest in new inventory.

**Strategic Allocation**: You can choose which pieces to bring to which events based on the audience. Younger demographic event? Bring more entry-level sterling pieces. Affluent audience? Bring higher-end gold and diamond pieces. You're not spreading inventory thin across all channels—you're concentrating it where it's most likely to sell.

**Real-Time Adjustment**: If a particular piece is selling fast at an event, you can feature it more prominently. If something isn't moving, you can adjust your pitch or offer a special price. You have control and flexibility that you don't have with online sales.

**Reduced Risk of Overstock**: Because you're getting immediate feedback on what sells, you're less likely to order large quantities of pieces that don't resonate. The market tells you directly what to reorder.

**Order-Taking Capability**: If you sell out of a popular item at an event, you can take orders with a deposit and ship after you restock. This actually helps you gauge demand and plan inventory more accurately.

The 6-week production turnaround is a constraint, yes. But events help you manage that constraint by providing clear, immediate data on what's selling so you can make smart reorder decisions.

## Part A: Major National Christian Women's Conferences

### Understanding the National Conference Landscape

Before diving into specific conference recommendations, it's important to understand what "major national Christian women's conferences" actually means and why they represent both tremendous opportunity and significant investment.

These are large-scale gatherings—typically 1,000 to 65,000 attendees—organized by established ministries, publishers, or parachurch organizations. They feature well-known speakers (authors, Bible teachers, worship leaders), professional production quality, and attendees who travel from across the country (or even internationally) to participate. Women don't stumble into these conferences casually; they register months in advance, pay $100-300+ for tickets, book hotels, arrange childcare, and clear their calendars. They're invested—financially, emotionally, and spiritually.

That level of investment creates a unique environment for 31:33 Jewelry:

**Concentrated Target Audience**: These aren't general women's events with some Christians in attendance. These are explicitly faith-focused gatherings where 100% of attendees are believers seeking to deepen their relationship with God. Your entire audience is pre-qualified.

**Receptive Mindset**: Women attend conferences in a posture of openness. They're expecting to encounter God, learn something new, and invest in resources that will help them grow spiritually. They're not in "scroll past ads" mode—they're in "what can I take home from this experience?" mode. That includes tangible reminders like jewelry.

**Gift-Buying Opportunity**: Many women attend conferences with friends, daughters, mothers, or small group members. They're thinking about gifts for the women in their lives. A piece of jewelry that represents belonging and covenant? That's not just a purchase—it's a meaningful gift they can give to someone they love.

**Social Proof and FOMO**: When women see others at their table or in their small group purchasing your jewelry, it creates social proof. "If she's buying it, maybe I should look at it too." The concentrated environment amplifies this effect in ways that online shopping never can.

**Content and Connection**: Conferences are highly social. Women take photos, post on Instagram, share what they're learning and experiencing. If they purchase your jewelry at the event, they're likely to post about it, tag your brand, and share the story with their followers. Instant user-generated content and word-of-mouth marketing.

The flip side of this opportunity is the investment required. Booth fees for major national conferences range from $2,000 to $15,000+. Add travel, lodging, meals, and the opportunity cost of 3-5 days away from other business activities, and you're looking at a significant commitment. That's why the strategic recommendation is to **start with smaller regional events first**, prove the model, build confidence and systems, and *then* scale to major national conferences once you have data showing they'll be profitable.

But when you're ready—when you've tested at 5-10 smaller events and know your booth setup works, your pricing resonates, and your sales process converts—major national conferences become a powerful accelerator for brand awareness and revenue growth.

## Part G: Networking & Relationship Building

Events aren't just about selling jewelry to attendees. The real long-term value often comes from the *people you meet* at events.

### Who to Connect With

**1. Ministry Leaders & Speakers**

If you're at a conference where Christine Caine or Jennie Allen is speaking, you probably won't get face time with them at your booth. But you might meet their team members, ministry coordinators, or event organizers. These are valuable connections.

**What to do**: Introduce yourself briefly. "Hi, I'm Dana with 31:33 Jewelry. We create faith-inspired jewelry based on Jeremiah 31:33. I'd love to send you a piece as a gift—can I get your info?" Follow up after the event with a handwritten note and a piece of jewelry. This builds goodwill and potential future partnerships.

**2. Boutique Owners & Retailers**

Many boutique owners attend Christian conferences looking for products to carry. They'll stop at your booth, ask about wholesale terms, and request a catalog.

**What to do**: Have a wholesale one-sheet ready (wholesale pricing, minimum order, terms). Collect their business card. Follow up within one week with: "It was great meeting you at [Event]. Here's our wholesale catalog. Let's schedule a call to discuss how 31:33 could fit in your store."

**3. Content Creators & Influencers**

You'll meet bloggers, podcasters, and Instagram influencers at events. They're often looking for brands to feature.

**What to do**: Offer to gift them a piece in exchange for an honest review or feature. "I'd love to send you one of our pieces. If you love it and it resonates with your audience, we'd be honored if you'd share about it." No pressure, just genuine relationship.

**4. Other Vendors**

The vendor next to you might sell Christian apparel, home decor, or books. Don't see them as competition—see them as potential partners.

**What to do**: Exchange products. "I'll trade you a necklace for one of your t-shirts." Wear their product, they wear yours. Cross-promote on social media. Refer customers to each other.

### The Long Game

Most event networking doesn't pay off immediately. You meet someone, exchange info, follow up, and maybe six months later they email: "Hey, I run a women's retreat with 500 attendees. Would you be interested in being our vendor?"
