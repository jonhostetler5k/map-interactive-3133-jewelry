# Church & Ministry Partnerships Playbook

## Overview & Strategic Rationale

Church partnerships represent one of the most natural fits for 31:33 Jewelry. Unlike retail channels where you're competing for shelf space and attention, churches provide direct access to concentrated audiences of believers who already understand covenant theology and are actively seeking ways to express their faith.

**Why This Channel Fits 31:33's Current Stage:**

### Direct Access to Your Core Audience

When you walk into a Christian bookstore, you're hoping the right customer happens to visit that day. When you partner with a church, you're reaching hundreds of believers gathered in one place, already in a spiritually focused mindset. A women's ministry event with 80 attendees isn't just 80 potential customers—it's 80 women who chose to spend their Saturday morning deepening their faith. These are exactly the people who will connect with the Jeremiah 31:33 covenant promise.

The trust factor is built-in. When a women's ministry director says, "We're offering this beautiful jewelry that reminds us we belong to God's family," that carries exponentially more weight than any advertisement. The church has already done the hard work of building credibility and relationship. You're simply providing a meaningful product that serves their community.

### Multiple Partnership Models That Work With Your Constraints

Your current inventory situation—5 to 45 pieces per style with 3-6 week turnaround—makes some marketing channels impractical. But church partnerships are remarkably flexible. You can work with what you have right now:

A women's retreat needs 25 pendants as gifts for attendees? That's a single bulk order you can fulfill from current inventory. A youth group wants to raise $3,000 for a mission trip? Provide 20 pieces on consignment for a four-week campaign. A large church has a bookstore? Start with 12 pieces and restock based on what sells.

Unlike retail stores that expect consistent inventory across dozens of SKUs, churches typically work with you on specific events or focused campaigns. This event-based model aligns perfectly with your production capacity. You're not promising to keep shelves stocked year-round—you're fulfilling discrete orders tied to specific ministry needs.

### Scalable From Single Church to Denominational Partnerships

You can start small and local. Reach out to three churches in your area this month. Test different partnership models. Learn what works. Then expand concentrically—more churches in Florida, then the Southeast, then strategic national opportunities.

As you build a track record, you're not just adding individual church partnerships. You're creating case studies and testimonials that open doors to denominational relationships. When you can show the women's ministry coordinator at a Baptist denominational office that you've successfully partnered with 15 Baptist churches with 70% reorder rates, you're positioned for a much larger conversation about rolling out across their network of hundreds of churches.

### Building Blocks for Larger Opportunities

Every church partnership generates assets beyond immediate revenue. You're collecting testimonials from ministry leaders. You're gathering photos of real women wearing 31:33 at retreats and events. You're building relationships with influencers in the Christian community who can open doors you don't even know exist yet.

When you eventually approach Christian retailers or larger organizations, you'll have proof points: "We've partnered with 30 churches across 8 states. We've provided jewelry for women's retreats serving over 500 attendees. Ministry leaders consistently tell us this jewelry sparks meaningful conversations about faith and belonging."

That's a completely different conversation than "We have a great product and think churches would like it."

### What Success Looks Like

**Phase 1 (Months 1-3)**: 3-5 pilot church partnerships testing different models (bulk orders, fundraising, consignment). You're learning what works, developing systems, and gathering initial testimonials. Revenue goal: $15,000-20,000.

**Phase 2 (Months 4-6)**: 10-15 total churches with refined processes and proven partnership models. You know which approaches generate the best results and can replicate them efficiently. Revenue goal: $40,000-50,000.

**Phase 3 (Months 7-12)**: 25-30 churches including some denominational connections. You're attending ministry conferences, getting referrals from satisfied partners, and building toward sustainable channel revenue. Revenue goal: $100,000-120,000.

### Key Distinction from Other Channels

This section focuses on partnerships with church organizations—not individual believers who happen to attend church. We're talking about churches as institutional partners who purchase, promote, or sell 31:33 jewelry to their congregations and communities.

This is distinct from individual customer acquisition (covered in online marketing sections), boutique retail partnerships (covered separately), and conference vendor relationships (also separate). The unique element here is leveraging the church's existing community, credibility, and communication channels to reach believers at scale.



## Part A: Understanding Church Partnership Models

Before you start reaching out to churches, you need to understand the different ways churches can partner with 31:33. Each model has distinct advantages, financial structures, and ideal use cases. The right model depends on the church's size, infrastructure, and ministry needs—not what you prefer to sell.

### Partnership Model #1: Bulk Orders for Ministry Events

This is often the simplest entry point and the easiest model to execute. A church is planning a specific event—a women's retreat, a baptism celebration, a Mother's Day service—and wants to provide meaningful gifts to participants. They purchase jewelry from you in bulk at wholesale pricing, then distribute it as gifts or sell it at cost to attendees.

**How It Works in Practice**

The women's ministry director contacts you (or you reach out to her) about an upcoming retreat. She has 60 women registered and wants to give each attendee a pendant as a retreat gift. You propose the "I AM HIS" pendant at wholesale pricing: $150 retail becomes $75 wholesale. Total order: $4,500.

You send an invoice with net 30 payment terms (or prepayment for first-time partners). She pays, you ship the order two weeks before the retreat, and she distributes the pendants during the event. The church isn't trying to make money on this—they're providing a meaningful gift that reinforces the retreat theme of belonging to God.

**Financial Structure**

Wholesale pricing typically runs 40-50% off retail. For 31:33, given your current margins, you'd likely offer 50% off retail for bulk orders. This means:

- $150 retail pendant → $75 wholesale
- $300 retail necklace → $150 wholesale  
- $800 retail bracelet → $400 wholesale

Your gross margin per piece drops significantly compared to direct sales, but you're moving volume with a single transaction and minimal marketing cost. A 20-piece order generates $1,200 gross margin (assuming $60 margin per piece after COGS). That's comparable to selling 20 pieces individually online, but accomplished through one relationship and one shipment.

Minimum order quantities keep transactions worthwhile. For Phase 1, set a 10-piece minimum for first orders. As you scale and refine operations, you might increase this to 15 or 20 pieces to ensure each partnership justifies the time investment.

Payment terms matter. Smaller churches (under 200 weekly attendance) should prepay or work on net 15 terms. They often operate on tight budgets and slower payment processes. Medium to large churches (200+) can typically handle net 30, and established partners might get net 45 once you've built trust.

**Best Fit For**

Churches with active women's ministry programs hosting regular events. Look for churches that already invest in their women's ministry—they're hosting retreats, conferences, Bible studies with childcare, and special events. These churches understand the value of providing quality resources and gifts to participants.

Churches with 200+ weekly attendance have the scale to make bulk orders worthwhile. A church with 50 members might love your jewelry, but they're unlikely to order 20+ pieces for an event. Focus your outreach on churches large enough to generate meaningful order volume.

Churches hosting specific events with clear timelines. Retreats, baptism celebrations, confirmation ceremonies, Mother's Day services, Christmas gifts for volunteers—these are all natural opportunities for bulk jewelry orders. When you can tie your product to a specific event the church is already planning and budgeting for, the conversation becomes much easier.

### Partnership Model #2: Fundraising Partnerships

Fundraising partnerships flip the financial model. Instead of the church purchasing jewelry as gifts, they sell jewelry to their congregation with a markup, keeping a portion of sales as fundraising revenue. This works beautifully for churches with active fundraising needs—mission trips, building campaigns, ministry expansions.

**How It Works in Practice**

A youth group is raising $5,000 for a summer mission trip. The youth pastor contacts you about a fundraising campaign. You provide 40 pieces of jewelry on consignment (mix of styles and price points). The youth group promotes the jewelry for four weeks via church announcements, social media, and a display table in the lobby on Sundays.

Youth group members serve as "brand ambassadors," wearing the jewelry and sharing the story with congregation members. When someone purchases a $300 necklace, the church keeps $120 (40%) and you receive $180 (60%). At the end of the campaign, you reconcile sales, collect payment for your portion, and retrieve any unsold inventory.

If they sell 25 pieces averaging $300 retail, that's $7,500 in total sales. The church keeps $3,000 for their mission trip. You receive $4,500, which after COGS gives you roughly $1,500 gross margin. The church raised significant funds without upfront investment, and you moved inventory while building a relationship.

**Financial Structure**

Fundraising partnerships typically work on consignment with a revenue split. The church takes 40-50% of retail sales, you receive 50-60%. This is more generous to the church than standard retail consignment (where stores typically take 40%) because churches are doing the promotional work and you're supporting their ministry mission.

Two common structures:

**60/40 split (you receive 60%)**: This works well when you're providing significant support—marketing materials, training, potentially attending events to help sell. The church keeps 40% of sales as fundraising revenue.

**50/50 split**: This is appropriate when the church is doing most of the heavy lifting—they're promoting heavily, they have motivated volunteers selling, and you're primarily providing inventory. The equal split feels fair and generous to ministry leaders.

You can also offer wholesale purchase with the church taking full margin. They buy 30 pieces at 50% off retail ($4,500 investment), then sell at full retail ($9,000), keeping the $4,500 difference. This requires the church to commit capital upfront, which many can't or won't do, but it simplifies your involvement—you get paid immediately and don't need to track consignment inventory.

**Best Fit For**

Churches with active fundraising needs and upcoming campaigns. Youth groups raising money for mission trips are ideal—they're motivated, they have a clear goal, and they're willing to actively promote. Women's ministry groups funding conference attendance, missions committees supporting missionaries, building campaigns—any ministry with a specific fundraising target.

Churches with engaged congregations willing to support fundraising. Some churches have cultures where members regularly support fundraising efforts (buying cookie dough, attending fundraising dinners, purchasing products). These churches are perfect for jewelry fundraising. Churches where fundraising efforts typically fall flat probably aren't good candidates.

Churches with 4-6 week campaign timelines. Fundraising partnerships work best as focused campaigns, not ongoing programs. A four-week push with clear start and end dates creates urgency and focus. Longer campaigns lose momentum; shorter campaigns don't give enough time for word to spread.

### Partnership Model #3: Church Gift Shop/Bookstore

Larger churches often have established bookstores or gift shops serving their congregation and visitors. These operate like boutique retail partnerships (covered in that section) but with the built-in advantage of a faith-aligned customer base and mission-driven environment.

**How It Works in Practice**

A church with 800 weekly attendance has a bookstore in their lobby, staffed by volunteers and open before and after services. The bookstore manager is always looking for meaningful products that align with the church's values. You reach out proposing a consignment partnership.

You start with 15 pieces—a mix of your most popular styles at various price points. The bookstore displays them near the checkout counter with your story cards explaining Jeremiah 31:33. When pieces sell, the bookstore keeps their percentage (typically 40-50%) and remits the remainder to you monthly or quarterly.

This is an ongoing relationship, not a one-time event. You check in monthly to see what's selling, restock when inventory drops below 50%, and adjust your product mix based on what resonates with their customers. Over time, you learn that this particular church's members prefer simpler styles at lower price points, so you adjust accordingly.

**Financial Structure**

Consignment arrangements typically split 60/40 or 50/50. You retain ownership of the inventory, the church displays and sells it, and you split revenue when pieces sell.

**60/40 split (you receive 60%)**: Standard for consignment when you're providing marketing support, training materials, and regular communication. The church keeps 40% for their effort in displaying, selling, and managing transactions.

**50/50 split**: Appropriate for high-traffic locations where the church is doing significant promotional work or when you want to be especially generous to support their ministry.

Wholesale purchase is also an option for established bookstores with purchasing budgets. They buy inventory at 50% off retail, own it outright, and manage it like any other product line. This is cleaner operationally (no consignment tracking) but requires the church to commit capital upfront.

Inventory reconciliation happens monthly or quarterly. You send a simple email: "According to my records, you currently have these 12 pieces in your bookstore. Can you confirm this matches your inventory?" This catches discrepancies early and maintains accurate tracking.

**Best Fit For**

Larger churches (500+ weekly attendance) with established bookstores or gift shops. Smaller churches rarely have the infrastructure or foot traffic to make ongoing retail partnerships worthwhile. Focus on churches with dedicated retail space, regular operating hours, and paid or committed volunteer staff.

Churches with physical retail space and consistent staffing. A bookstore that's only open one hour on Sunday mornings won't generate enough sales to justify the partnership. Look for bookstores open multiple days per week or at minimum before and after all weekend services.

Churches in high-traffic locations or serving as tourist destinations. Some churches attract visitors beyond their regular congregation—historic churches, churches in downtown areas, churches known for specific ministries. These locations provide exposure beyond the immediate church family.

### Partnership Model #4: Pastor/Ministry Leader Endorsement

Not every church has the infrastructure for retail or the bandwidth for fundraising campaigns. But nearly every church has communication channels—Sunday announcements, email newsletters, social media, bulletin inserts. The endorsement model leverages these channels without requiring the church to handle inventory or sales.

**How It Works in Practice**

A pastor personally connects with the 31:33 story. Maybe he receives a sample piece, reads about the Jeremiah 31:33 covenant promise, and feels it aligns with his church's teaching on identity in Christ. He agrees to endorse 31:33 to his congregation.

On Mother's Day weekend, he mentions from the pulpit: "If you're looking for a meaningful gift for your wife or mother, I want to recommend 31:33 Jewelry. Each piece is built on Jeremiah 31:33—God's covenant promise that we belong to Him. It's a beautiful way to express faith through something tangible."

The church includes a blurb in their email newsletter with a link to your website and a church-specific discount code: GRACECHURCH15. Congregation members who purchase use that code, which tracks sales back to this partnership. At the end of the quarter, you calculate total sales from that code and send the church a check for 15-20% as an affiliate commission.

The church didn't handle any inventory, process any transactions, or manage any logistics. They simply shared something meaningful with their community. You gained access to their congregation, and they received passive revenue that can support their ministry.

**Financial Structure**

Affiliate commission typically runs 15-20% of sales. This is lower than consignment splits because the church isn't handling inventory, managing sales, or taking any risk. They're simply promoting your product to their audience.

**15% commission**: Standard for straightforward endorsement where the pastor mentions it once or twice and includes it in communications.

**20% commission**: Appropriate when the church is actively promoting—multiple announcements, featured in newsletters, social media posts, potentially a dedicated table with information (but not inventory).

You track sales via unique discount codes or affiliate links. Each church gets their own code (CHURCHNAME15 or similar). Your e-commerce platform tracks which sales used which codes, making commission calculation straightforward.

Payment happens quarterly or when commission reaches a minimum threshold ($100-200). This keeps administrative overhead manageable while ensuring churches receive meaningful revenue.

**Best Fit For**

Smaller churches (under 200 attendance) without retail infrastructure. These churches want to serve their members but don't have bookstores or the capacity to manage fundraising campaigns. Endorsement gives them a way to participate without operational burden.

Churches with strong digital engagement and communication. If a church has an active email list, engaged social media following, and members who pay attention to church communications, endorsement can be highly effective. Churches where announcements go unnoticed probably won't generate significant results.

Pastors or ministry leaders who personally connect with your brand story. Authentic endorsement requires genuine belief in what you're doing. When a pastor truly loves the Jeremiah 31:33 message and sees how it aligns with his teaching, his endorsement carries weight. Forced or transactional endorsements fall flat.



## Part B: Identifying and Vetting Potential Partners

Now that you understand the partnership models, the next step is finding the right churches to approach. The goal isn't to partner with as many churches as possible—it's to find churches where your mission aligns, your product fits their community, and both parties can build a sustainable relationship.

### Target Church Profiles

Not every church is a good fit for 31:33. You're looking for specific characteristics that indicate a church will value what you offer and has the capacity to partner effectively.

**Church Size Matters, But Not How You Think**

The sweet spot for Phase 1 partnerships is churches with 200-1,000 weekly attendance. These churches are large enough to generate meaningful order volume but small enough that you can build personal relationships with decision-makers. A church with 400 members hosting a women's retreat might have 60-80 attendees—perfect for a bulk order. Their women's ministry director can make decisions without navigating layers of bureaucracy.

Churches over 1,000 attendance (megachurches) can be valuable partners, but they often have established vendor relationships, formal purchasing processes, and longer decision timelines. Save these for Phase 2 or 3 when you have case studies and proven results to present. The exception: if you have a personal connection to a megachurch leader, pursue it. Relationships trump process.

Smaller churches under 200 attendance can work well for endorsement partnerships where they're simply promoting to their congregation without handling inventory. They're unlikely to generate bulk orders large enough to justify the effort, but a pastor who genuinely loves your brand can influence his entire congregation through authentic endorsement.

**Denominational Alignment**

Some denominations naturally align with 31:33's theology and aesthetic better than others. Evangelical denominations with strong emphasis on covenant theology and personal faith are your primary targets: non-denominational churches with contemporary worship, Baptist (especially Southern Baptist and independent Baptist), Pentecostal and Assemblies of God, Nazarene, Christian & Missionary Alliance, and Bible churches.

These denominations typically emphasize personal relationship with God, understand covenant language, and have active women's ministry programs. Their congregations are accustomed to expressing faith through tangible means—Christian books, worship music, faith-based products.

Catholic parishes can be excellent partners for sacramental gifts. Baptism, first communion, and confirmation are significant milestones where families purchase meaningful gifts. A Catholic bookstore or gift shop might carry 31:33 as a confirmation gift option. The covenant theology translates well, though you'll want to ensure your messaging respects Catholic tradition.

Mainline Protestant denominations (Episcopal, Presbyterian, Methodist, Lutheran) vary widely. Some churches in these denominations are theologically conservative and mission-focused, making them great partners. Others are more liturgical and less focused on contemporary faith expressions. Research individual churches rather than assuming based on denomination alone.

**Ministry Program Indicators**

The best church partners have active ministry programs that create natural opportunities for 31:33. Look for churches with robust women's ministry programs—regular Bible studies, annual retreats, conferences, and special events. These churches already invest in their women and understand the value of providing quality resources.

Churches with active missions programs often have fundraising needs. Youth groups raising money for mission trips, missions committees supporting missionaries, short-term mission teams funding travel—these are all opportunities for fundraising partnerships.

Churches with established bookstores or gift shops have retail infrastructure and are accustomed to carrying products. They understand inventory management, have point-of-sale systems, and already serve their congregation through retail.

Churches hosting regular events create recurring opportunities. A church that hosts quarterly women's events, annual retreats, monthly prayer breakfasts, or seasonal celebrations provides multiple touchpoints throughout the year rather than a single one-time opportunity.

### Discovery Methods

Finding the right church partners requires some research, but it's more straightforward than you might think. Start with what's closest and most accessible, then expand outward.

**Start Local and Personal**

Your first partnerships should be geographically close. You're in Florida, so begin there. Local partnerships are easier to manage—you can visit in person, drop off inventory yourself, attend events to support your partners, and troubleshoot issues face-to-face.

Google "churches near me" and filter by denomination or size. Visit church websites to assess their ministry programs, values, and culture. Look for churches with professional websites, active social media, and clear information about their ministries. These operational indicators suggest a church that's organized and capable of partnering effectively.

Attend services at churches that seem like good fits. There's no substitute for experiencing a church firsthand. You'll get a sense of their culture, their congregation's demographic, and whether your jewelry would resonate with their community. After the service, introduce yourself to ministry leaders. Mention that you have a faith-based jewelry brand and would love to explore partnership opportunities.

If you're already part of a church community, start there. Your own church knows you, trusts you, and understands your heart for ministry. They're often willing to be your first pilot partner. Even if they're not a perfect fit size-wise, the relationship and trust make them valuable for testing your approach.

**Leverage Ministry Networks and Directories**

Denominational websites maintain lists of affiliated churches by region. If you identify a denomination that aligns well with 31:33, visit their national or regional website. Most have church locator tools where you can search by state or city. This gives you a targeted list of churches that share theological alignment.

Christian conference centers often publish lists of churches that attend their events or are part of their network. If you identify a conference center as a potential partner (covered in the Christian Conferences & Events section), ask for their mailing list or network of affiliated churches. Churches that invest in sending members to Christian conferences are exactly the churches that will value 31:33.

Women's ministry networks and organizations like Proverbs 31 Ministries, She Reads Truth, or IF:Gathering have communities of churches engaged in women's ministry. While you may not get direct access to their church lists, you can engage with their content, attend their events, and connect with ministry leaders who are part of these networks.

Faith-based business directories and Christian Chamber of Commerce chapters can connect you with church leaders who are also business owners or who support Christian businesses. These relationships can open doors to church partnerships.

**Referrals and Word of Mouth**

Once you establish your first few church partnerships, ask for referrals. Ministry leaders often know each other—they attend the same conferences, participate in denominational networks, and connect through local pastor gatherings. A warm introduction from one women's ministry director to another carries far more weight than a cold email.

When a church partnership goes well, explicitly ask: "Do you know other churches or ministry leaders who might be interested in partnering with 31:33? We'd love a warm introduction if you're willing to make one."

Your customers can also be referral sources. When someone purchases from you online, include a note in their package: "Do you have a church or ministry that would love 31:33? We'd be grateful for an introduction." Customers who love your brand are often happy to connect you with their church leaders.

### Vetting Process

Not every church that seems like a good fit will actually be a good partner. Before investing time in outreach and relationship-building, do some basic vetting to ensure alignment and viability.

**Theological Alignment Comes First**

Visit the church's website and read their statement of faith or beliefs page. You're looking for alignment on core theology: the authority of Scripture, salvation through Jesus Christ, and the personal nature of relationship with God. The Jeremiah 31:33 covenant promise should resonate with their theological framework.

Red flags include prosperity gospel extremes (health and wealth as primary focus), cult-like behavior or authoritarian leadership, theological positions that contradict basic Christian orthodoxy, or values that conflict with 31:33's mission. If something feels off theologically, trust that instinct and move on. There are thousands of churches that align well—you don't need to force partnerships with churches that don't.

**Assess Financial Stability and Professionalism**

A church doesn't need to be wealthy to be a good partner, but they do need to be financially stable and professionally operated. Churches that have been established for 3+ years have proven they can sustain themselves. Brand new church plants might be wonderful communities, but they're often operating on shoestring budgets and may not have capacity for partnerships.

Professional communication matters. When you reach out via email or phone, do they respond promptly and professionally? Is their website current and well-maintained? Do they have clear contact information for ministry leaders? These operational indicators suggest a church that will follow through on commitments and pay invoices on time.

Active ministry programs indicate financial health. Churches investing in women's ministry, missions, youth programs, and events are churches with resources. They're not just keeping the lights on—they're actively serving their community.

**Evaluate Decision-Maker Accessibility**

Some churches have simple decision-making processes where a women's ministry director can approve a $2,000 bulk order without extensive committee approval. Other churches require proposals to go through multiple committees, budget reviews, and board approvals that take months.

During initial conversations, ask about their decision-making process: "If we wanted to move forward with a partnership, what would that process look like on your end?" If the answer involves six months of committee meetings, that church might not be a good fit for Phase 1. Save them for later when you have more established credibility and can justify the longer timeline.

Look for churches where you can build direct relationships with decision-makers. If every conversation has to go through administrative gatekeepers and you never actually speak with the women's ministry director or bookstore manager, the partnership will be difficult to nurture.

**Cultural Fit and Aesthetic Alignment**

Visit the church's social media and look at photos from their events. Does the aesthetic align with 31:33's brand? You're looking for churches with contemporary, thoughtful aesthetics—not necessarily trendy, but intentional about how they present themselves.

Churches that value quality over bargains are better fits. If a church's entire culture is built around thrift and frugality (which can be wonderful in its own right), they may struggle with 31:33's price points. You need churches that understand the value of investing in meaningful, quality items.

The congregation's demographic should match your target customer. Churches with strong representation of women ages 25-55 are ideal. Churches that skew very young (college ministries) or very old (aging congregations) may not have as many women in your core demographic.

**Trust Your Instincts**

Data and checklists are helpful, but intuition matters too. If something feels off about a church—the leader seems flaky, the communication is unprofessional, the theology raises questions—it's okay to pass. You're building long-term relationships, not just making transactions. The wrong partnership can waste time, damage your brand reputation, and create frustration.

Conversely, when you find a church where everything clicks—the mission aligns, the leaders are genuine and enthusiastic, the congregation is engaged—lean into that relationship even if it doesn't check every box on paper. Some of your best partnerships will come from churches that genuinely love what you're doing and become advocates for your brand.



## Part C: Outreach and Partnership Development

You've identified potential church partners and vetted them for alignment. Now comes the outreach phase—making contact, presenting your brand, and developing partnerships that work for both parties. The key is being confident but collaborative, personal but professional.

### Initial Contact Strategy

Cold outreach to churches is different from cold outreach in other contexts. Ministry leaders are busy serving their congregations, and they receive countless vendor pitches. Your initial contact needs to stand out by demonstrating genuine understanding of their ministry and authentic alignment with their mission.

**Identifying the Right Contact Person**

Before you reach out, identify who actually makes decisions about the partnership you're proposing. For bulk orders related to women's events, contact the women's ministry director or coordinator. For bookstore partnerships, reach out to the bookstore manager or retail coordinator. For fundraising campaigns, the youth pastor (for youth group fundraisers) or missions pastor (for missions fundraising) is your contact. For endorsement partnerships, you may need to reach the senior pastor directly, though his assistant can often facilitate the introduction.

Most church websites list staff with contact information. If you can't find the right person, call the church office and ask: "I have a faith-based jewelry brand and I'm interested in exploring partnership opportunities. Who would be the right person to speak with about women's ministry events?" The receptionist will point you in the right direction.

**Crafting Your Outreach Email**

Generic mass emails get deleted immediately. Your email needs to feel personal and demonstrate that you've done your homework about this specific church. Here's a framework that works:

**Subject line**: Keep it specific and relevant. "Faith-based jewelry for [Church Name] women's retreat" or "Partnership opportunity for [Church Name] missions fundraising" works better than "Wholesale jewelry opportunity."

**Opening**: Reference something specific about their church or ministry. "I was on your website yesterday and saw that you're hosting a women's retreat in March. The theme of 'Finding Your Identity in Christ' resonates deeply with our brand's mission." This immediately shows you're not sending a generic pitch to 50 churches.

**Your story (brief)**: "I'm Dana, founder of 31:33 Jewelry. Our brand is built on Jeremiah 31:33—God's covenant promise that we belong to Him. Every piece we create is designed to spark conversations about faith and belonging." Keep this to 2-3 sentences. They can learn more if they're interested.

**Why you're reaching out**: "I think 31:33 would be meaningful for your retreat attendees because [specific reason based on their retreat theme or ministry focus]. Our pieces are fine jewelry—real gold, real silver, real diamonds—with minimalist aesthetics that communicate faith without being overly religious in appearance."

**Specific proposal**: Don't be vague about what you're offering. "I'd love to discuss providing pendants as retreat gifts for your attendees at wholesale pricing, or setting up a consignment display if you prefer women to purchase their own pieces." Give them something concrete to respond to.

**Call to action**: "Would you be open to a 15-minute call next week to explore this? I'm happy to send our line sheet first if you'd prefer to review our products before we talk." Offer options and make it easy to say yes.

**Closing**: "Thank you for the work you do serving women in [city/community]. I look forward to connecting." Acknowledge their ministry and express genuine appreciation.

**Following Up Without Being Pushy**

Ministry leaders are busy. If you don't hear back within a week, a polite follow-up is appropriate. Send a brief email: "Hi [Name], I wanted to follow up on my email from last week about 31:33 Jewelry. I know you're busy serving your congregation, so no pressure at all. If this isn't the right time or fit, I completely understand. Just wanted to make sure my email didn't get buried."

If you still don't hear back after a second email, move on. They're either not interested, too busy, or it's not the right timing. You can try again in 6 months, but don't keep pestering.

**Phone Outreach**

For local churches, a phone call can be more effective than email. Call during office hours (Tuesday-Thursday, 10am-3pm tends to be best—avoid Mondays when staff are recovering from the weekend, and Fridays when they're preparing for Sunday).

When you call, be direct and respectful of their time: "Hi, my name is Dana and I'm the founder of 31:33 Jewelry, a faith-based jewelry brand. I'm reaching out to churches in [area] about partnership opportunities. Is [ministry leader name] available, or would it be better to send an email first?"

If they're available and willing to talk, have your pitch ready but keep it conversational. If they're not available, ask for their email and mention you'll send information they can review at their convenience.

**In-Person Visits**

For churches in your local area, nothing beats showing up in person. Attend a Sunday service as a visitor. Experience their worship, listen to the teaching, get a feel for the community. After the service, ministry leaders are often accessible in the lobby or at a welcome table.

Introduce yourself genuinely: "Hi, I'm Dana. I'm new to the area and really enjoyed the service this morning. I also have a faith-based jewelry brand called 31:33, and I'd love to explore partnership opportunities with your women's ministry. Would you be open to a conversation about that?"

Bring a sample piece in a nice presentation box. Let them hold it, see the quality, read the story card. Jewelry is tactile—experiencing it in person creates a completely different connection than seeing photos online.

### Pitch Materials and Brand Presentation

When a church expresses interest, you need professional materials that tell your story and make it easy for them to say yes.

**One-Page Partnership Overview**

Create a single-page document (PDF) that outlines partnership options. Include your brand story (2-3 sentences about Jeremiah 31:33 and the covenant promise), partnership models available (bulk orders, fundraising, consignment, endorsement), pricing structure for each model, and contact information.

Keep the design clean and aligned with your brand aesthetic. This isn't a sales brochure—it's a ministry resource that helps them understand how 31:33 can serve their community.

**Product Line Sheet**

Your line sheet should include high-quality photos of each style you're offering for church partnerships (you don't need to show every SKU—focus on the 5-10 styles most appropriate for churches), product details (style name, materials, dimensions), retail and wholesale pricing clearly marked, and minimum order quantities if applicable.

For church partnerships, you might create a simplified line sheet featuring your most meaningful pieces rather than your entire catalog. A women's ministry director doesn't need to see 19 styles—she needs to see the 5 styles that will resonate most with her retreat attendees.

**Brand Story Document**

Some ministry leaders want deeper context before partnering. Create a 1-2 page document that includes your testimony (how God gave you the vision for 31:33), the meaning of Jeremiah 31:33 and the covenant promise of belonging, who wears 31:33 and why it resonates, and what makes your jewelry different (fine materials, minimalist aesthetic, conversation-starting design).

This document helps ministry leaders understand they're not just adding another product—they're partnering with a mission-driven brand that aligns with their values and will resonate with their community.

**Sample Pieces**

For in-person meetings or local partnerships, bring 2-3 physical samples. Include your most popular styles at different price points—maybe an entry-level pendant at $150, a mid-range necklace at $300, and a higher-end piece at $600-800. This shows the range and quality.

Include story cards with each sample explaining what 31:33 means. Let decision-makers hold the pieces, feel the weight and quality, and imagine their women wearing them. This tactile experience often closes the deal more effectively than any pitch.

**Ministry Leader Talking Points**

Create a simple one-page guide for how ministry leaders can introduce 31:33 to their congregation. Include suggested announcement language ("We're excited to offer 31:33 Jewelry at our upcoming retreat. Each piece is inspired by Jeremiah 31:33—God's covenant promise that we belong to Him..."), key talking points (fine jewelry quality, designed as conversation starters, minimalist aesthetic, heirloom quality), and how to answer common questions ("What does 31:33 mean?" "Is this real gold/silver?" "Can I see different styles?").

This makes it easy for busy ministry leaders to promote your jewelry effectively without having to craft their own messaging.

### Negotiation and Partnership Terms

Once a church is interested, you'll need to agree on terms that work for both parties. Approach this as collaborative problem-solving, not adversarial negotiation.

**Pricing and Payment Terms**

For bulk orders, offer wholesale pricing at 50% off retail. A $150 pendant becomes $75 wholesale, a $300 necklace becomes $150. This is standard and expected.

For high-volume orders (50+ pieces), you might offer 55% off retail as an incentive. This rewards churches that are making significant commitments and helps you move more inventory.

Payment terms depend on church size and your relationship. First-time partners or smaller churches should prepay or work on net 15 terms. Established partners or larger churches can work on net 30 or net 45. Be clear in your invoice: "Payment due within 30 days of invoice date."

**Consignment and Fundraising Splits**

For consignment partnerships (bookstores or fundraising campaigns), propose a 60/40 split with you receiving 60%. This is fair given that you're providing inventory and support materials.

If the church pushes back or you want to be especially generous to support their ministry, offer 50/50. This equal split feels fair to ministry leaders and demonstrates that you're genuinely interested in supporting their mission, not just making sales.

For fundraising campaigns where the church is doing heavy promotional work, 50/50 is often the right starting point. They're investing time and energy into selling your jewelry—the equal split acknowledges that contribution.

**Minimum Order Quantities**

For Phase 1, keep minimums low to make partnerships accessible. A 10-piece minimum for bulk orders ensures the transaction is worthwhile without being prohibitive for smaller churches.

For consignment or fundraising, you might not require minimums at all. Starting with 12-15 pieces gives you data on what sells without over-committing inventory.

As you scale and refine operations, you can increase minimums (15-20 pieces) to ensure each partnership justifies the time investment.

**Trial Periods and Flexibility**

Propose a 90-day trial for new partnerships, especially consignment relationships. "Let's start with 90 days and see how it goes. At the end of that period, we'll review sales data and decide together whether to continue and potentially expand."

This de-risks the relationship for both parties. The church isn't locked into a long-term commitment with an unproven brand, and you're not stuck with inventory in a location that isn't selling.

For event-based partnerships (bulk orders, fundraising campaigns), the trial is built-in. One retreat or one fundraiser gives you both data to decide whether to work together again.

**Protecting Your Brand**

As you negotiate, don't compromise on things that protect your brand integrity. Establish a Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) policy—all partners must advertise your jewelry at agreed retail prices. They can offer discounts at checkout, but advertised prices stay consistent.

Provide approved marketing language and ask partners to run any promotional copy by you first. You want to ensure the Jeremiah 31:33 message is communicated accurately and respectfully.

Request that your jewelry be displayed in ways that align with your brand—not mixed with costume jewelry or discount bins, displayed with good lighting and your story cards visible.

**Finding Win-Win Terms**

The best partnerships feel fair to both parties. If a church is hesitant about your proposed terms, ask: "What would make this partnership work well for you?" Maybe they need longer payment windows, or they want to start with fewer pieces, or they prefer different styles than you initially proposed.

Be flexible where you can without compromising your core needs (fair margins, brand protection, operational feasibility). The goal is a sustainable partnership where both parties feel valued and excited about working together.

**Getting It in Writing**

Once you've agreed on terms, put them in a simple written agreement. This doesn't need to be a complex legal contract—a one-page document outlining the key terms is sufficient for Phase 1.

Include: partnership model (bulk order, consignment, fundraising, endorsement), pricing and payment terms, commission splits if applicable, trial period duration, inventory reconciliation process for consignment, and return/exchange policy.

Both parties sign and date it. This protects everyone and ensures there's no confusion later about what was agreed upon. If either party wants to modify terms, you can always amend the agreement in writing.



## Part D: Operational Implementation

Once you've secured a church partnership, the real work begins. This section covers the practical systems you need to manage inventory, support your partners, and ensure smooth operations.

### Inventory Management for Church Partnerships

With limited inventory (5-45 pieces per style), smart allocation is critical. You need to balance church partnerships with direct online sales while ensuring you can fulfill commitments.

**Allocation Strategy**

Reserve 20-30% of your total inventory for church partnerships. This ensures you have product available when opportunities arise without starving your direct sales channel. If you have 40 pieces of a popular pendant style, allocate 8-12 pieces for church partnerships and keep 28-32 for online sales.

For specific events with confirmed orders, allocate inventory as soon as the partnership is finalized. If a church orders 25 pendants for a March retreat, mark those 25 pieces as allocated in January when they confirm. This prevents you from accidentally selling them online before the retreat.

Prioritize proven partners and large orders. A church that's ordering 50 pieces for a major event takes priority over a new consignment partnership requesting 10 pieces. The larger order moves more inventory and generates more revenue.

**Tracking System**

For Phase 1 with 3-5 church partners, a simple spreadsheet works perfectly. Create columns for: Church Name, Partnership Type, Pieces Allocated, Date Allocated, Event Date (if applicable), Retail Value, Your Revenue, Payment Status, and Notes.

Update this weekly based on new partnerships, sales reports from consignment partners, and completed orders. When you scale to 10+ partnerships, consider inventory management software, but don't over-engineer it early on.

**Reorder Management**

Establish clear reorder triggers with each partner. For consignment partnerships, agree on a restock point: "When you've sold 60% of the initial inventory, let me know and I'll send more pieces." This prevents stockouts while giving you lead time to produce replacements.

For bulk order partners, follow up 60-90 days after their event: "How did the retreat go? Are you planning another event where 31:33 might be a good fit?" This proactive outreach often leads to reorders before they even think to contact you.

Track seasonal patterns. If women's retreats typically happen in spring and fall, plan your inventory production accordingly. Build up stock in January-February for spring retreats, and again in July-August for fall events.

### Supporting Your Church Partners

Your church partners need resources to effectively promote and sell 31:33. The easier you make it for them, the more successful the partnership will be.

**Marketing Materials for Churches**

Create ready-to-use announcement templates for church bulletins: "We're excited to offer 31:33 Jewelry at our upcoming women's retreat. Each piece is inspired by Jeremiah 31:33—God's covenant promise that we belong to Him. These beautiful pendants will serve as lasting reminders of what we'll learn together about our identity in Christ."

Provide email newsletter copy that churches can drop into their communications: "Looking for a meaningful gift for Mother's Day? We're partnering with 31:33 Jewelry to offer fine jewelry that celebrates faith and belonging. Visit [link] to see the collection and use code [CHURCHNAME] for [X]% off."

Design social media graphics that churches can post on Instagram and Facebook. Include high-quality product photos with text overlays explaining the Jeremiah 31:33 message. Make these available as downloadable files they can use immediately.

**Display and Merchandising Guidance**

For bookstore consignment partnerships, provide simple display recommendations. 31:33 pieces should be displayed together (not scattered among other brands), positioned at eye level when possible, with good lighting to make the jewelry sparkle, and story cards placed visibly next to each piece.

If budget allows, provide simple display materials—small velvet display pads, acrylic stands, or a small branded sign explaining "What does 31:33 mean?" This ensures consistent presentation across all church partners.

**Training Materials**

Create a one-page training sheet for church staff and volunteers who will be selling or discussing 31:33. Include the core message (Jeremiah 31:33 covenant promise of belonging), key talking points (fine jewelry quality, conversation starters, minimalist aesthetic, heirloom quality), and answers to common questions.

For fundraising campaigns, provide talking points for youth group members or volunteers who are actively selling: "This isn't just jewelry—it's a daily reminder that you belong to God's family. Every time someone asks what 31:33 means, you get to share your faith."

### Event Support and Engagement

For local church partnerships, your personal involvement can significantly boost results and strengthen relationships.

**Attending Events When Possible**

If a church is hosting a women's retreat within driving distance and you're providing jewelry for attendees, offer to attend. You don't need to speak or present—simply being there to meet women, answer questions, and share your testimony creates powerful connections.

When you can't attend in person, record a short video message (2-3 minutes) that the church can play during their event. Share your story of how God gave you the vision for 31:33, explain the Jeremiah 31:33 covenant promise, and express gratitude for their partnership.

**Co-Marketing on Your Channels**

Feature your church partners on your own social media and website. Post on Instagram: "Excited to partner with [Church Name] for their upcoming women's retreat! If you're in the [city] area and looking for a church home, check them out." This drives awareness to their ministry and strengthens the relationship.

Create a "Find 31:33 in Your Area" page on your website listing all church bookstores and retail partners. This drives foot traffic to their locations and gives them additional value from the partnership.

Share photos from church events (with permission) on your social channels. Women wearing 31:33 at a retreat, a display in a church bookstore, a fundraising table in a church lobby—these authentic images build credibility and attract other church partners.



## Part E: Relationship Management and Growth

Church partnerships aren't "set it and forget it." They require ongoing attention, but the time investment is manageable and pays dividends in loyalty and repeat business.

### Ongoing Communication

**Monthly Check-Ins**

For active partnerships (consignment bookstores, recent bulk orders, ongoing fundraising), reach out monthly via email or quick phone call. Keep it brief and focused: "How are the pieces performing? Any customer feedback? Do you need additional inventory or support materials?"

This keeps you top-of-mind, surfaces issues early, and shows you care about their success. A 15-minute call once a month prevents problems from festering and often uncovers new opportunities.

**Quarterly Business Reviews**

Every 90 days, have a more substantive conversation with each partner. Review sales performance (which styles sold, which didn't), discuss sell-through rates and what's working, gather customer feedback and common questions, and explore opportunities to expand or adjust the partnership.

Use this data to make decisions. Should you add more inventory? Switch to different styles? Transition from consignment to wholesale? Introduce them to a new product line? These quarterly reviews provide the insights you need to optimize each partnership.

**Responsive Support**

When partners reach out with questions or issues, respond within 24 hours. Ministry leaders are busy—if you're hard to reach or slow to respond, they'll deprioritize your brand and focus on vendors who are more accessible.

Even if you don't have an immediate answer, acknowledge their message: "Got your email about the inventory discrepancy. I'm looking into it and will have an answer for you by tomorrow afternoon." This builds trust and demonstrates professionalism.

### Performance Monitoring

Track key metrics for each partnership to identify what's working and what needs adjustment.

**Sell-Through Rate**

For consignment partnerships, calculate: (Units sold ÷ Units placed) × 100. Target 60%+ sell-through within 90 days. If a bookstore is at 40% after 90 days, investigate why. Wrong styles? Poor display? Pricing issues? Lack of promotion?

**Reorder Frequency**

Strong partnerships reorder regularly. Bulk order partners should come back for their next event (typically every 6-12 months). Consignment partners should reorder every 60-90 days as inventory sells. No reorders after 120 days signals a problem worth investigating.

**Average Order Value**

Track the typical order size for each partnership type. Are bulk orders averaging $3,000? $5,000? Are consignment placements selling $2,000 worth of jewelry per quarter? Understanding these benchmarks helps you forecast revenue and identify underperforming partnerships.

**Customer Feedback**

What are church staff hearing from customers? Positive feedback validates the partnership. Consistent questions or objections reveal opportunities to improve messaging, adjust pricing, or offer different styles.

### Scaling Strategy

**From Pilot to Program (Months 3-6)**

After your first 90 days with 3-5 pilot partners, evaluate what's working. Which partnerships have strong sell-through and positive feedback? What have you learned about which styles sell best in church contexts? Are you ready to add 5-7 more partners?

Only scale if pilots are successful. If sell-through is weak or partnerships are operationally challenging, fix those issues before expanding. It's better to have 3 great partnerships than 10 mediocre ones.

**Geographic Expansion**

Start local (Florida), then expand concentrically. Months 1-3 focus on local Florida partnerships where you can visit in person. Months 4-6 expand to the Southeast region (Georgia, Carolinas, Tennessee). Months 7-12 pursue strategic national opportunities (major Christian conference centers, high-profile churches, denominational connections).

This keeps logistics manageable and allows you to visit partners when needed. Local partnerships also generate referrals to other nearby churches.

**Building Champion Relationships**

As you scale to 10-15 partnerships, identify your champion churches—the top 20% by volume, the ministry leaders who personally love your brand, the churches that actively refer others. Give these champions special attention: monthly personal check-ins from Dana, first access to new product launches, featured prominently on your website and social media, and potential for exclusive styles or early releases.

These champions become your advocates, referring other churches, providing testimonials, and serving as case studies when you approach larger opportunities.

When churches see that respected Christian conferences feature 31:33 as a vendor, it adds credibility. Conference attendees bring 31:33 back to their churches, creating warm leads for church partnerships. Mention conference presence in church outreach: "We've partnered with [Conference Name] and would love to serve your church too."

**Affiliate & Referral Programs**

Church partners can also be affiliate partners for online sales. If a bookstore doesn't carry a particular style in-store, they can refer customers to your website with their affiliate code and earn commission. This creates incentive to promote your full line, not just what they stock.

### Synergistic Marketing Opportunities

**Email Marketing**

Feature church partners in your email newsletters: "Visiting Orlando? Stop by [Church Name] bookstore to see 31:33 in person!" This drives foot traffic to partners and strengthens relationships. Churches appreciate the exposure, and it demonstrates you're invested in their success.

When you post about a church partnership, tag the church. When they post about 31:33, share it to your audience. This mutual promotion expands reach for both parties and builds goodwill.

**Seasonal Campaigns**

Coordinate with church partners around key seasons. Before Mother's Day, reach out to all church partners: "Mother's Day is coming up. Would you like to promote 31:33 as a gift option? Here are ready-to-use graphics and announcement templates." Churches get easy content, you get increased sales.

### Timeline and Phasing

Add 5-7 additional partnerships based on pilot learnings, bringing total to 10-15 churches. Refine materials and processes based on feedback from Phase 1. **Phase 3: Strategic Expansion (Months 7-12)**

Grow to 25-30 total church partnerships. Expand geographically beyond local market into regional and strategic national opportunities. Implement partner tier system (champions, core partners, new partners). Generate $100,000-120,000 in church channel revenue. ## Part H: Practical Use Cases and Examples

Real-world scenarios help you visualize how church partnerships work in practice. Here are four detailed use cases covering different partnership models.

### Use Case #1: Women's Retreat Bulk Order

**Scenario**: Grace Community Church (500 weekly attendance) hosts an annual women's retreat with 75 attendees. The retreat theme is "Rooted in Christ" focusing on identity and belonging.

**Partnership Approach**

You discover this church through local research and notice their retreat is scheduled for April. You reach out to the women's ministry director in January, three months before the event. You propose providing "I AM HIS" pendants as retreat gifts for all attendees at wholesale pricing.

The director loves the connection between the retreat theme and the Jeremiah 31:33 message. She requests 75 pendants at $75 wholesale each (retail $150). Total order: $5,625. You offer net 30 payment terms given the church's size and established reputation.

**Execution**

You send an invoice in February when she confirms the order. She pays in early March. You ship 75 pendants in individual gift pouches with story cards two weeks before the retreat. You also provide a 3-minute video message from Dana that they play during a session, explaining the 31:33 covenant promise.

The retreat happens in April. Women receive the pendants during a closing session focused on remembering their identity in Christ. Many wear them immediately. Several post photos on social media, tagging 31:33 and the church.

**Expected Outcome**

$5,625 revenue, $2,250 gross margin (assuming $30 COGS per piece). 75 women wearing 31:33, creating brand awareness in the community. Testimonials and photos from the retreat for your marketing. Strong relationship with the women's ministry director who refers you to two other churches. Annual reorder opportunity for next year's retreat.

### Use Case #2: Youth Group Missions Fundraiser

**Scenario**: Cornerstone Church youth group (30 students) is raising $6,000 for a summer mission trip to Guatemala. They need creative fundraising ideas beyond the typical car wash and bake sale.

**Partnership Approach**

The youth pastor contacts you (or you reach out to him) about a fundraising campaign. You propose a 4-week consignment campaign where the youth group sells 31:33 jewelry to the congregation, keeping 50% of sales as fundraising revenue.

You provide 35 pieces on consignment—a mix of styles and price points from $150-$600, weighted toward $200-$400 pieces that are accessible but meaningful. Total retail value: $10,500.

**Execution**

You deliver the inventory to the church and set up a display table in the lobby. Youth group members serve as "brand ambassadors," wearing the jewelry and sharing the story with congregation members before and after services for four Sundays.

The youth pastor promotes the fundraiser from the pulpit and in the church newsletter. You provide social media graphics and email templates they use to promote. Several youth group parents purchase pieces as Mother's Day gifts (campaign happens in April-May).

Over four weeks, they sell 22 pieces totaling $6,600 in sales. The church keeps $3,300 for the mission trip. You receive $3,300, which after COGS gives you approximately $1,320 gross margin. You retrieve the 13 unsold pieces to use in other partnerships.

**Expected Outcome**

$3,300 revenue, $1,320 gross margin. Youth group raises half their mission trip funding through one campaign. 22 congregation members now wearing 31:33, creating ongoing awareness. Strong relationship with youth pastor who refers you to the youth ministry network in his denomination. Potential for annual fundraiser partnership.

### Use Case #3: Church Bookstore Consignment

**Scenario**: First Baptist Church (1,200 weekly attendance) has an established bookstore in their lobby, open before and after all weekend services and Wednesday evenings. The bookstore manager is always looking for meaningful products that align with the church's values.

**Partnership Approach**

You reach out proposing a consignment partnership. The bookstore manager agrees to a 90-day trial with 15 pieces. You propose a 60/40 split (you receive 60%) given that you'll provide marketing support and regular communication.

You select 15 pieces representing your most popular styles at various price points: 5 pieces at $150-200, 7 pieces at $250-350, and 3 pieces at $400-600. Total retail value: $4,500.

**Execution**

You deliver the inventory in person, set up a small display near the checkout counter, and train the bookstore staff on the 31:33 story. You provide story cards for each piece and a small sign: "What does 31:33 mean?"

You check in monthly via email to see what's selling. After 30 days, 4 pieces have sold ($1,100 total sales). After 60 days, 5 more pieces have sold ($1,600 additional sales). After 90 days, total sales are $3,200 (11 pieces sold, 73% sell-through).

The church keeps $1,280 (40%). You receive $1,920, which after COGS gives you approximately $660 gross margin. You restock with 11 new pieces to replace what sold, and the partnership continues beyond the trial period.

**Expected Outcome**

$1,920 revenue per quarter, $660 gross margin per quarter. Strong 73% sell-through rate validates the partnership. Ongoing relationship with stable quarterly revenue. Church bookstore earns $1,280 per quarter to support their ministry. Potential to expand to more pieces or transition to wholesale as the relationship matures.

### Use Case #4: Pastor Endorsement + Affiliate

**Scenario**: New Life Church (180 weekly attendance) is too small for bulk orders and doesn't have a bookstore, but the pastor personally connects with the 31:33 message after receiving a sample piece.

**Partnership Approach**

You send the pastor a sample "31:33" pendant with a personal note explaining the brand story. He wears it for several weeks and multiple people ask about it. He reaches out expressing interest in sharing 31:33 with his congregation.

You propose an endorsement partnership with affiliate commission. The church will promote 31:33 via announcements, newsletter, and social media. I've been wearing this pendant for a few weeks, and it's sparked so many great conversations about faith. Each piece is based on Jeremiah 31:33—God's covenant promise that we belong to Him."

$3,600 revenue, $1,800 gross margin. Church receives $720 passive income with zero inventory risk or operational burden. 12 congregation members now wearing 31:33. Potential for ongoing quarterly promotions (Mother's Day, Christmas, Easter). Pastor becomes an authentic advocate who refers other pastors in his network.
